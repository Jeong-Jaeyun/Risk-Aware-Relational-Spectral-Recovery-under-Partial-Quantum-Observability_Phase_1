\title{Risk-Aware Template-Switching Control under Partial Quantum Observability}

\author{Jaeyun Jeong$^1$\orcid{0009-0005-3281-5858} and Hwa Young Jeong$^2$\orcid{0000-0002-5017-934X}}

\keywords{template switching; partial observability; finite-clock model; state restoration; quantum information; risk-aware control}

\begin{abstract}
With only partial observations, a discrete reference template can fit the
available data yet still be a harmful choice.  We ask whether a test based on
observed data can filter such template switches without access to the target
state.  An audit of our submitted three-qubit simulations found a degenerate
clock-conditioned representation and a veto driven solely by an uncertainty
threshold.  We retain those results as an audit, without attributing their
effect to relational spectral structure.  R1 establishes an energy-matched
finite-clock construction.  R3 compares a continuous estimate with a direct
nearest-template estimate and tests score, uncertainty, and held-out residual
switching rules.  Both candidates are fitted on four clock labels and scored
unchanged on the other four.  We evaluate one corrected run and three independent
replications, each with separate reference-bank, calibration, and test sets.
Because any one-sided veto reduces the unconditional number of harmful
switches, we additionally compare selection quality at equal expected
switching rates.  In a reviewer-prompted analysis of the same four streams,
retaining half of the score-only proposals by held-out residual lowers the
harmful fraction among accepted switches by 20.8--23.7 percentage points
relative to uncertainty ranking.  The combined gate has a smaller,
operating-point-dependent benefit.  A continuous estimator optimized using
all measurements nevertheless achieves higher grid-mean fidelity than these
switching rules.  The contribution is a controlled assessment of selection
information and measurement cost in finite-data template switching.  The clock
specifies a physically consistent observation model; neither its necessity for
the decision task nor a relational-spectral performance advantage is established.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Quantum error correction relies on diagnostic information that can be
incomplete or noisy.  Syndrome-based correction, recovery maps, autonomous
protection, and error mitigation address different parts of this problem
\cite{Lautenbacher2024PetzRecoveryQudit,Gertler2021AutonomousQECBosonic,VanDenBerg2023SparsePauliLindbladPEC,Strikis2021LearningBasedQEM,Liao2024MachineLearningQEM}.
Finite-shot state estimation raises a related decision question: when should a
policy replace a continuous estimate with a discrete reference template?  The
template may fit the available counts yet yield lower target fidelity.  We test
whether counts withheld from fitting can filter these harmful switches.  This
decision test does not measure the efficacy of a syndrome decoder.

The submitted work proposed to derive a switching signal from clock-conditioned
graph spectra.  The audit reported here shows why that interpretation fails:
the conditional density is constant, and the admissibility condition never
binds.  We document the submitted construction and test a held-out residual
gate using an energy-matched finite clock and finite-shot counts.

Graph and spectral representations remain useful descriptive tools for the
submitted audit~\cite{Braunstein2006GraphLaplacianDensityMatrix,Adhikari2017WeightedDigraphsQuantumStates,Biamonte2019ComplexNetworksQuantum}.
The R3 safety test instead asks whether a template fitted on four clock labels
predicts the other four as well as a continuous estimate.  This finite-data
decision is complementary to characterization and uncertainty quantification
\cite{Hashim2025BenchmarkingCharacterization,Cieslinski2024RandomisedMeasurements,Bennink2026UncertaintyQuantificationQC}.
It does not evaluate a syndrome decoder or channel-level mitigation method.

R3 evaluates this question in a controlled encoded logical-qubit testbed.
Candidate \(A\) is a continuous Bloch-ball projected least-squares estimate and
candidate \(B\) is the direct nearest template under the same fitting-label
loss.  A policy may accept or veto a proposed switch from \(A\) to \(B\).  The
held-out gate can only veto a proposal; it cannot create a template switch.  The
comparison therefore exposes both harmful-switch reduction and any loss of mean
fidelity relative to the continuous candidate.

R1 supplies an energy-matched finite-clock construction with nonconstant
conditional states.  R3 tests the switching decision with physical code noise,
direct-template and uncertainty-only controls, and a corrected held-out
residual gate.  Each of its four fixed streams has separate bank, calibration,
and test targets; uncertainty intervals resample target pairs and measurement
seeds.  The residual gate reduces harmful switches relative to score-only
selection in every evaluated cell, but the subset relation alone cannot
establish useful selection.  R4 therefore compares the same records at equal
expected switching rates and adds globally optimized continuous-loss
estimators.  This distinguishes information about proposal quality from a
reduction in intervention frequency.

The contribution is a controlled benchmark of a specific decision: whether
to replace a fitting-stage continuous estimate by a fixed-bank template.
The held-out residual contains selection information beyond random rejection
and uncertainty in this testbed, while the full-measurement continuous control
exposes the cost of reserving data for that decision.  R1 supplies the
physically consistent observation model needed for the adopted clock
interpretation; it is not a separate performance contribution.
The validation principle is ordinary data splitting, applied here to a
specified quantum measurement model with explicit candidate, information,
and switching-rate controls.

\section{Background and Positioning}
\subsection{Clock-conditioned relational dynamics}

Relational formulations of quantum dynamics describe change through correlations
with an internal clock rather than an externally supplied time parameter. This
idea appears in background-independence and relational-dynamics
discussions~\cite{Smolin2005BackgroundIndependence,Rovelli2007QuantumGravity}.
The Page--Wootters mechanism gives the operational ingredient used here: a
constrained global state can be conditioned on a clock subsystem to obtain
relative system states~\cite{Giovannetti2015QuantumTime,Marletto2017EvolutionWithoutEvolution,Hoehn2021TrinityRelationalQuantumDynamics}.
Related work has studied robustness, interactions, noncausal circuits,
measurement-related time flow, and computational uses of quantum
time~\cite{Rijavec2023RobustnessPageWootters,Mendes2021NonlinearPageWootters,Baumann2022NoncausalPageWoottersCircuits,Paiva2022FlowTimeEnergyMeasurements,Diaz2025ParallelPageWoottersSimulation}.
Our use of this literature is narrow.  R1 uses clock-conditioned slices to
construct a finite observation model, and R3 uses its labels to split fitting
and validation records.  The switching rules operate on the resulting count
record.  An equivalent randomized-setting experiment has the same likelihood,
as shown in Section~\ref{subsec:clock-role}; the internal-clock interpretation
is not required by the statistical decision rule.

\subsection{Spectral graph representations of quantum structure}

Graph-based and spectral representations provide compact descriptions of relational 
structure. In quantum information, graph Laplacians have been related to density matrices, 
separability, and quantum-state representations~\cite{Braunstein2006GraphLaplacianDensityMatrix,Adhikari2017WeightedDigraphsQuantumStates,Bradshaw2023GraphZetaEntanglement}. 
More broadly, quantum network models provide a language for connecting graph structure, 
correlations, and quantum dynamics~\cite{Biamonte2019ComplexNetworksQuantum}. These works 
motivate the use of spectral signatures as auditable summaries of relational connectivity.

The submitted work differed from graph-state or network modeling approaches in its
use of clock-conditioned spectral families. A single graph spectrum was not the
central object; each internal clock label produced a relational graph, and the
ordered family of Laplacian spectra defined a relational spectral trajectory.
The revision retains that construction only to make the failed submitted-grid
mechanism auditable. It is not an evidence source for R3.

\subsection{Quantum recovery, mitigation, and control}
Quantum recovery and error mitigation have developed along several complementary directions. 
Recovery-map approaches formalize channel-level reconstruction and approximate reversibility~\cite{Lautenbacher2024PetzRecoveryQudit}, 
while quantum error-correction experiments and autonomous protection schemes demonstrate 
how encoded or stabilized systems can preserve quantum information under noise~\cite{Reed2013EntanglementQEC,Gertler2021AutonomousQECBosonic,Nascimento2011NoiselessSubsystem}. In the NISQ setting, mitigation methods such as probabilistic error cancellation, virtual distillation, detection-based mitigation, and learning-based correction address noisy circuit outputs without requiring full fault tolerance~\cite{VanDenBerg2023SparsePauliLindbladPEC,Xu2024CircuitNoiseResilientVirtualDistillation,Zhang2021AutoencoderErrorMitigation,Strikis2021LearningBasedQEM,Liao2024MachineLearningQEM,Liao2025NoiseAgnosticQEM}.

The submitted framework placed a policy layer above two candidate recovery
mechanisms.  In those encoded-QEC simulations, syndrome-first recovery was the
decoder-facing baseline, while relational admissible recovery supplied an
alternative candidate.  R3 asks a different, narrower question: it compares a
continuous count-record estimate with a direct template under equal information
and uses held-out counts to test the proposed switch.

\subsection{Partial observability, uncertainty, and benchmarking}

Reliable recovery decisions depend on how much diagnostic information is available and 
how trustworthy that information is. Benchmarking and characterization methods provide 
tools for quantifying device behavior and validating performance claims~\cite{Hashim2025BenchmarkingCharacterization}. 
Randomized-measurement techniques and uncertainty-quantification frameworks also emphasize 
that finite, noisy, or incomplete observations must be interpreted with calibrated uncertainty~\cite{Cieslinski2024RandomisedMeasurements,Bennink2026UncertaintyQuantificationQC}. 
Noise modeling, live noise fingerprinting, robust control, and non-Markovian noise studies 
further show that quantum behavior can depend strongly on the structure and timing of noise 
processes~\cite{Ji2026DataEfficientNoiseModeling,Bensoussan2025LiveNoiseFingerprinting,Ding2025UniversallyRobustControl,Cai2020RandomTelegraphDephasing,Zhang2024NonMarkovianTeleportation,Zhuang2026FluxoniumNonMarkovian}.

The submitted grid tested candidate selection when syndrome records were
degraded, using a simple syndrome-stress index.  Those diagnostics are retained
for the audit.  R3 instead tests template switching with finite-shot counts and
a held-out residual.  Its uncertainty statistic is a thresholded proxy, not a
calibrated posterior.  Both analyses report harmful switches and beneficial
switches that the policy blocks.

\section{Submitted-implementation audit}\label{sec:results}

The submitted implementation is retained as an audit, with its full
construction, penalties, metrics, and regime tables in Supplementary
Sections S1--S10.  It used $H_S=0$ together with a clock initialization
and Hamiltonian that produced constant normalized conditional system
densities.  Its nominal joint state also failed the stated zero-energy
constraint.  Consequently, those simulations did not instantiate the
nondegenerate relational trajectory required by the proposed interpretation.

The submitted admissibility gate was nonbinding in every evaluated row.
After removing the uncertainty conjunct, the score/admissibility-only
counterfactual vetoed no proposed switches.  Historical improvements in
downside metrics therefore arose from the uncertainty-threshold veto and
cannot be attributed to relational-spectral admissibility.  Tables S11--S14
retain the intervention counts, uncertainty intervals, and row-level effects
so that this withdrawal is traceable.

The candidate comparison also had asymmetric information: the submitted
syndrome-first candidate used the complete recovery channel, while degraded
syndrome symbols changed controller support rather than the candidate itself.
It was therefore not a damaged-syndrome decoder benchmark.  R1 and R3 replace
these premises with a valid finite observation model and candidates fitted
from identical finite-shot counts.  R4 then asks whether the observed gate
effect survives an explicit comparison at equal switching rates.

\section{Observation model and template-switching assessment}\label{sec:revision-results}

\subsection{Physically specified finite-clock construction (R1)}
\label{subsec:clock-role}

The revision experiments use an encoded logical qubit and an energy-matched
two-level clock, rather than the submitted $H_S=0$ setup.  Let
\begin{equation}
H_C=\omega Z_C,\qquad H_S=-\omega(n_xX_L+n_zZ_L),\qquad \omega=0.8,
\end{equation}
where the tilted setting is $n=(1,0,1)/\sqrt{2}$.  If $|n_+\rangle$ and
$|n_-\rangle$ are the logical eigenstates of $n_xX_L+n_zZ_L$ and
$|\psi\rangle=c_+|n_+\rangle+c_-|n_-\rangle$, we prepare
\begin{equation}
|\Psi\rangle=c_+|0\rangle_C|n_+\rangle_L+c_-|1\rangle_C|n_-\rangle_L,
\label{eq:revision-history-state}
\end{equation}
which obeys $(H_C+H_S)|\Psi\rangle=0$.  Eight clock effects
$E_k=(2/8)|\tau_k\rangle\langle\tau_k|$ at
$\tau_k=k\pi/(8\omega)$ form the finite POVM.  R1 verifies the null constraint,
conditional evolution, POVM completeness, nondegenerate clock geometry, and the
rank-three tilted observation design.  It is an observability qualification, not
a QEC efficacy result.

The physical role of this construction must be separated from the statistical
role of the gate.  For the population-count readout used here, the likelihood
of clock label $k$ and outcome $z\in\{-1,+1\}$ can be written
\begin{equation}
p(k,z\mid\boldsymbol r)
=\frac{1}{16}\left[1+z(1-2\eta)\boldsymbol d_k^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol r\right],
\label{eq:clock-likelihood}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol r$ is the initial logical Bloch vector, $\eta$ is the
known readout-flip probability, and $\boldsymbol d_k$ includes the declared
rotation and physical noise.  Selecting one of eight ordinary measurement
settings uniformly and measuring its corresponding logical population yields
the same likelihood.  Thus energy matching is required for our chosen
stationary-state clock interpretation, but no clock-specific statistical
advantage or operational necessity follows.  Graph spectra do not enter R3
or R4.

\subsection{Corrected held-out template-switching risk test (R3)}

R3 uses four fixed data streams, each with disjoint reference-bank,
calibration, and held-out target sets.  Stream~0 reuses R2's random streams to
isolate the gate-definition correction; streams~1--3 use independent bank,
calibration, target, and measurement streams.  The switching policy receives
finite-shot count records, the known dynamics, and a fixed 32-state bank.  Four
clock labels are used for fitting, and the other four for validation.
The bank, calibration, and test targets are pure logical-qubit states.
Dynamics, physical noise, and readout-flip probabilities are known to the
estimators.  Candidate $A$ is a continuous Bloch-ball projected weighted least-squares
estimate; candidate $B$ is the direct nearest template under the same
fitting-label loss.

Let $L_{\mathrm{val}}(X)$ be the weighted squared loss of the unchanged
fitting-label candidate $X$ on the validation-label counts.  The held-out
residual statistic is
\begin{equation}
h=L_{\mathrm{val}}(B_{\mathrm{fit}})-L_{\mathrm{val}}(A_{\mathrm{fit}}).
\end{equation}
Neither candidate is refit on validation labels.  Score-only $C_2$,
uncertainty-only $U$, held-out-residual-only $H$, and the combined gate $C_3^R$
are evaluated on identical records.  For each stream, the score, uncertainty,
and residual thresholds are set once to the 75th empirical quantiles of that
stream's separate calibration set at the fixed bit-flip, 1,024-copy,
physical-noise 0.05, readout-flip 0.05 operating point.  They remain fixed
across all 24 held-out test cells in that stream; their values may differ
between streams.  The target density is used only after a decision to calculate
fidelity.  An oracle nearest-template fidelity is reported only as an offline
bank-resolution
diagnostic, never as a policy input.

In every one of the 24 grid cells in each of the four streams, $H-C_2$ has a
negative harmful-switch-rate difference whose two-way target-pair/measurement-
seed 95\% interval excludes zero.  At the fixed middle operating point
(bit-flip, 1,024 prepared copies, physical-noise probability 0.05, and readout
flip 0.05), the $H-C_2$ differences are $-14.844$, $-8.333$, $-10.677$, and
$-5.990$ percentage points in streams~0--3.  The two-way interval for
$C_3^R-U$ is negative in 12, 8, 12, and 6 cells, respectively; the combined
gate therefore does not improve uniformly on uncertainty-only selection.  The
sign of the $C_3^R-A$ mean-fidelity difference also varies across streams.
Full results and intervals are
reported in the Supplementary Information.

These unconditional harmful-switch reductions are descriptive: $H$ accepts a
subset of the $C_2$ switches, and $C_3^R$ a subset of the $U$ switches.  They
cannot alone establish that the rejected proposals are selectively worse.
R4 addresses this issue directly.

\subsection{Selection quality at equal switching rates (R4)}
\label{subsec:r4}

R4 is a reviewer-prompted secondary analysis of the existing R3 streams.
Its analysis rules were fixed before computing the new comparisons, after
the original R3 outcomes were known.  It replays all 73,728 target/count
records from their recorded seeds and verifies agreement with the saved R3
diagnostics and fidelities; no new count realizations are introduced.
Within each cell, we first thin the original $C_2$, $H$, and $U$ decisions
uniformly to the expected number accepted by $C_3^R$.  Averaging over this
randomization gives identical expected switching rates.  Only the twelve
1,024-copy cells per stream have positive common coverage; at 256 copies,
the original $U$ and $C_3^R$ accept no switches.  The harmful fraction among
accepted switches, rather than its unconditional count, now measures selection
quality.  Paired two-way bootstrap samples recompute the thinning factors.

At common coverage, the stream-mean harmful fraction for $H$ is lower than
for uniformly thinned $C_2$ by 7.62, 3.00, 6.19, and 4.48 percentage points
in streams~0--3.  The corresponding $C_3^R-U$ reductions are 5.23, 1.46,
4.37, and 1.93 points.  All eight paired pointwise 95\% intervals exclude
zero (Supplementary Table S17).  These contrasts establish
selection information in the evaluated positive-coverage cells, beyond the
automatic effect of accepting fewer switches.

We also compare batch rankings that accept 25\%, 50\%, or 75\% of the
original $C_2$ proposals.  Scores are fitting loss difference, uncertainty
$u$, residual $h$, and the combined statistic
$\max(u/t_u,h/t_h)$ using the original R3 calibration thresholds.
Uniform random retention supplies a control.  Boundary ties are randomized
analytically, so expected switching rates match exactly in each cell and
resample.  Cutoffs use the batch's observed diagnostics without target
outcomes; these are batch selection curves, not newly calibrated deployment
rules.  Figure~\ref{fig:r4-ranking} shows that residual ranking is more
selective than uncertainty or their conjunction over all three retention
levels in the stream averages.  At 50\% retention, its $H-U$ harmful-fraction
differences are $-20.78$, $-21.76$, $-23.68$, and $-23.50$ percentage points,
with all four paired intervals below zero.

The numerical harm criterion $F_B<F_A-10^{-12}$ counts even very small
losses.  A follow-up sensitivity analysis keeps every decision rule fixed
and applies absolute fidelity margins 0.001, 0.005, and 0.01.
At margin 0.01 and 50\% retention, the $H-U$ harmful-fraction differences
are $-26.60$, $-24.08$, $-23.80$, and $-25.24$ percentage points in
streams~0--3, with all four paired intervals below zero.  Table S20 reports
every declared margin.  These margins assess sensitivity; they are not
validated application-specific utility thresholds.

The direct $H-U$ overall mean-fidelity differences at 50\% retention are
0.01084, 0.00977, 0.00748, and 0.01015, with all four paired intervals
above zero (Table S21).  These signed-fidelity comparisons retain all gains
and losses, irrespective of the harm margin.  They show value within the
specified batch-selection task, without implying the same gain for a
calibration-only deployment policy.

\subsection{Continuous estimation and the scope of template use}
\label{subsec:continuous-control}

The original $A$ radially projects an unconstrained weighted least-squares
fit onto the Bloch ball.  That projection need not minimize the constrained
loss.  R4 therefore adds its global constrained minimizer $A^*_{\rm fit}$
and a full-record control $A^*_{\rm all}$, both solving
\begin{equation}
\min_{\|\boldsymbol x\|\leq1}
\sum_{k\in\mathcal I} n_k
\left(\boldsymbol d_k^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol x-y_k\right)^2,
\label{eq:continuous-control}
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal I$ equal to the fitting labels or all eight labels.
The latter uses the same total prepared-copy budget as the residual gate
and accounts for the opportunity cost of holding measurements aside.
Feasibility, optimality conditions, and loss improvement over $A$ are checked
for every fit.  Optimization concerns this specified count loss, not the
unknown target fidelity.

At 50\% retention, the H ranking has higher grid-mean fidelity than
$A^*_{\rm fit}$ in each stream, with differences 0.00522, 0.00815, 0.00676,
and 0.00847 and paired intervals above zero.  However, its differences from
$A^*_{\rm all}$ are $-0.01195$, $-0.00897$, $-0.01000$, and $-0.00949$,
with intervals below zero (Supplementary Table S19).
Thus useful selection within a fitting-stage template task does not establish
an advantage over continuous estimation using all available measurements.

A discrete template is an optional, restricted output in this study.
A finite action alphabet could motivate such a decision in an application,
but we have not measured a hardware cost or downstream utility that would
justify that restriction.  When an unrestricted continuous estimate and
target fidelity are the only objectives, the full-record continuous control
is the supported choice on this grid.  The results characterize when residual
evidence helps select template proposals; they do not recommend template
substitution as a general recovery procedure.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The audit found that the submitted clock construction was degenerate and that
its reported veto effect could not be attributed to admissibility.  We retain
those results as an audit.  The revision adds an energy-matched finite-clock
construction and a template-switching experiment with separate calibration and
held-out target sets.

The supported result concerns selection quality in a specified two-candidate
task.  After matching expected switching rates, held-out residual evidence
selects fewer harmful template substitutions than random retention or
uncertainty alone in the reported stream averages.  This adds evidence beyond
the unconditional harmful-switch reduction guaranteed by a one-sided veto.

The extra complexity of the combined gate is therefore not generically earned
relative to uncertainty-only selection.  Across streams~0--3, its
unconditional harmful-switch-rate interval is strictly below that of the
uncertainty-only gate in only
12/24, 8/24, 12/24, and 6/24 evaluated cells, respectively, and its
mean-fidelity difference from the continuous estimate changes sign between
streams.  R4 finds a smaller but measurable conditional-quality benefit over
$U$ at matched coverage in the active 1,024-copy cells; nevertheless,
residual-only batch ranking is better than the combined ranking in the stream
averages.  These comparisons do not justify preferring the extra uncertainty
conjunct within the tested scaling family.

All-measurement continuous fitting gives higher grid-mean fidelity than the
tested switching rankings, so the study provides no general reason to prefer
a discrete template.  The finite clock supplies a consistent physical
realization of the count model, with no demonstrated necessity for the
selection rule.  The uncertainty statistic remains an uncalibrated proxy.
R4 reuses the four R3 streams of pure logical-qubit targets and its intervals
are pointwise and conditional on the fixed banks, known observation model, and
thresholds.  Mixed targets, model misspecification, out-of-grid generalization, an
application-specific reason for discrete outputs, and channel-level recovery
require separate evidence.
